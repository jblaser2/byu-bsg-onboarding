# Reading & Writing

Two habits separate people who are actually *doing research* from people who are just working:
reading regularly, and writing as you go rather than only at the end.

## Reading scientific articles

Reading papers is how you find out where the field actually stands right now — not where a
textbook says it stood a few years ago. It's how you avoid re-deriving something that's already
been solved, and how you find the methods and tools other people have already built for the
problem you're facing.

- **Start broad, then narrow.** Skim abstracts widely to get a feel for what's out there, then go
  deep on the handful that are actually relevant to your problem.
- **Where to look:** Google Scholar, PubMed, and preprint servers like arXiv and bioRxiv. Set up
  alerts for authors and topics you care about so new work finds you.
- **Follow the citations.** A good paper's reference list is often more useful than the paper
  itself — it's a reading list someone already built for you.
- **Keep a running note** of what you've read and the one-sentence takeaway. It's easy to read a
  paper, nod along, and forget it a week later. [Zotero](https://www.zotero.org/) is a good free
  tool for this — it organizes your papers and lets you take notes and highlight directly on them.
- Papers specific to our work are already collected on [Understanding CryoEM](cryoem.md) and in
  the [Learning Path's background reading](learning-path.md#simulation-biophysics-background-reading)
  — start there, then branch out.
- Make it a regular habit, not just something you do when you're stuck. A little every week beats
  a lot right before you need it.

## Writing — do it as you go

It's tempting to treat writing as the last step: do the research, *then* write it up. Don't. Write
about your research as you go — **writing about your research will guide what you do next.** The
act of putting what you're doing into words forces you to clarify your thinking, exposes gaps in
your reasoning you didn't notice, and helps you decide what actually matters. Writing isn't just a
record of the research — it's part of how the research gets done.

Some practical ways to do this:

- **Keep a running research log** as you work — what you tried, what happened, what you'd do
  differently next time. (See "Write things down" on [Tips & Tricks](tips.md).)
- **Draft as you go**, not from memory later. It's far easier to write down what you did today
  than to reconstruct it in six months when you're trying to write the paper.
- **When you're stuck, write the problem out in plain language.** Explaining it to a blank page
  (or to a person) often reveals what you're actually asking, or what you're missing.
- **Share drafts early and often.** A rough draft gives Dr. Hart and your teammates something
  concrete to react to — feedback on a bad draft is more useful than no feedback on a perfect idea
  you haven't written down yet.
- We write papers in [Overleaf using LaTeX](learning-path.md#6-overleaf-and-paper-writing) — see
  the Learning Path for the basics.

Together, reading and writing keep your research grounded: reading tells you where the field is;
writing tells you — and everyone else — where you actually are.
